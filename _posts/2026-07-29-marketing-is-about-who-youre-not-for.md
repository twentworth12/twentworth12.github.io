Marketing is also about communicating who you are not for.

Two stores in Nantucket I'd never heard of made the point better than a positioning workshop. Four Winds Craft Guild sells expensive shit, like $1000 picnic baskets, to the old-money rich. Dairy Boy is a fast-fashion popup that sells clothing to the young influencer crowd.

Four Winds put up a "no influencers" sign. It made the Dairy Boy owner and the Nantucket influencers big mad.

It's a simple business decision. Most of the old-money Nantucket crew is against influencers, and against anything that takes away from the rustic charm of the Island. The sign tells those customers this is a place they want to shop. A safe haven against the rest of the stores on the Island that cater to influencers. The crowd influenced by influencers isn't relevant to a store that sells random expensive arts and crafts. Four Winds didn't try to win that crowd on the way to selling a picnic basket. They told them the store wasn't theirs.

That's the message. Not a manifesto. A door. "No influencers" chooses, which is more than most marketing will risk. The customer Four Winds wants can walk past, see the sign, and feel relief. The store has picked a side in a fight that customer is already in. You don't get relief without a rejection attached. If the sign tried to welcome everyone, it would tell the old-money customer nothing except that this shop is like the other shops.

The people upset about the sign missed why it exists. It isn't a review of their careers. It is a marketing message to the customers Four Winds wants to attract and sell to. It's as simple as that. The sign is doing audience selection in public, which is what positioning is supposed to do before a single ad runs. Most of us hide that sentence in a doc nobody ships. They put it in the window.

I love creators, and we work with them at my company. I dream of being a creator someday. I might have missed the age window. None of that fights the sign. Wanting creators in the world is not the same as owing every influencer crowd a welcome. Four Winds looked at who they sell to, and at what that customer wants the Island to stay, and they printed the boundary. The mad crowd is the boundary working.

Marketers flinch here because the other instinct pays better in a planning meeting. Add a segment. Add a channel. Add a persona so the brief doesn't leave anyone out. The plan looks bigger. The store gets louder and less like itself. If you will not name who you're not for, you will write for a blur of everyone, and you will sound like every other company that was also afraid to leave someone out. Reach among people who will never buy the $1000 picnic basket is not demand. It's foot traffic through a store that just told its real customers the rustic charm is up for negotiation.

Who you're not for is a service, not an insult. It tells the right buyer they won't have to share the place with the thing they came to avoid. It tells your own team which ideas to decline. The collab that would spike attention and sand off the reason to shop there. The "they have reach" argument. Attention from the wrong room is a cost if it moves you toward a customer you don't want.

You can work with creators and still let a business say no. I do both. Four Winds made a choice a lot of brands rent an agency to avoid, and they fit it on a sign. I'd take that over another line about having something for everyone.

If your message never risks making the wrong person mad, it isn't finished. Write the sentence that would fit on a door. If you can't, you don't know who you're for yet.
