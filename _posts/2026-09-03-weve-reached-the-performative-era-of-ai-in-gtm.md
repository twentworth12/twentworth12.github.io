We've reached the performative era of AI in GTM.

By now, most companies have implemented a "context brain" that ties together and describes all your internal data, and have built agents and skills on top of that data. This has been the most important step change I've seen, and it has unlocked a ton of productivity improvements across GTM teams, including at incident.io.

That's the part worth doing. Get the company's knowledge into a form an agent can use. The product, the customers, the calls, the pricing, the things you will and won't say. If the agent can't see it, every prompt starts from zero and every draft sounds like a different company. Once the brain exists, the skills on top of it start to matter. People stop re-explaining the business to a chatbot and get back to the account, the page, and the campaign. That's a real change in how a GTM team spends its day. I have watched it happen.

Then the feeds caught up, and the feeds got weird.

All I read on LinkedIn and X now are performative use cases for AI. They don't seem to solve any real problems, other than showing off how great you are at deploying whatever harness the cool kids are talking about. Looking at you, Grok Bot.

A workflow that exists so you can post the workflow is not a go-to-market motion. It's a costume. Someone can wire up a new agent before lunch and still have nothing to say about whether a buyer is more likely to take the meeting. The build is the content. The business is somewhere else.

So here's the question those posts skip. Are these use cases really helping us grow faster?

If you can't answer that in plain language, you don't have an AI strategy. You have a hobby with a login. I'm not asking for a science project on attribution. I'm asking whether the work changed the speed of the team or the growth you're already on the hook for. Most of what I see skips that on purpose, because the demo is easier than the result. Anyone can ship a screenshot. Growing faster is the job.

Apple will have perhaps its most important launch ever next week, with iOS 27, AI Siri, a foldable iPhone, and likely a bunch of other rumored stuff. Apple isn't winning because they have the best AI CMO, or because they hired the most marketing engineers. They win because they a) deeply understand their customer, b) build fantastic products for them, c) are the best ever at communicating the value of those products, and d) have a tiering model that offers something at most every pricing level.

I never took marketing classes in college, but I think this sounds like the "4 p's of marketing." I'm as AI-pilled as it gets, but marketing is more than Clay waterfall enrichments or AEO automations in Profound.

Those tools are fine. A waterfall can hand you a contact. An automation can chase a mention. Neither one understands the customer, neither one builds the product, neither one writes the line a buyer remembers, and neither one decides what you offer and who it's for. You can automate all four of those into mush if you skip the thinking that makes them worth doing. Speed without a point of view is a faster way to sound like everyone else.

Unpopular opinion: I'll take Al Ries and Jack Trout, or the GOAT David Ogilvy, over a bunch of fancy marketing engineers any day, all day. Even in 2026.

Ries and Trout made you pick a place in the buyer's mind and give something else up to hold it. Ogilvy treated the ad as something that had to sell. I'll take that over a marketing engineer who can deploy every harness on the timeline and still can't say why anyone should care. Fancy is not a position. A position is a decision about the customer, the product, the words, and the offer.

The engineers I want close to marketing start there, then pick the tool. They point the context brain at a number the company already cares about. The performative version starts on X, grabs whatever harness people are screenshotting, and goes hunting for a problem it can star in.

Build the brain. Ask if it helps you grow faster. Then go back to Ries, Trout, and Ogilvy before you announce the next agent. The fundamentals are not a nostalgia act. They're the part the demo keeps skipping.
