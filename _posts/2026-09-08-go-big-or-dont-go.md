Event strategy is a binary choice. Go big, or don't go. Everything else is a story you tell after you've already spent the money.

A bunch of marketers I know, including me, were dunking on a well-funded Series A startup that spent a ton of money at Black Hat. A big booth. A bunch of activations around Vegas, including the biggest activation there is. We had a good time with it. Reflecting on the dunking, I was wrong.

That's what it takes to win at a massive event like Black Hat. As a startup, if you aren't willing to go big, it doesn't make sense to go at all.

We pulled out of Black Hat at my last company partly for that reason. We were a vendor with a booth, among dozens of other vendors with a booth. You can run that booth well and still be scenery. Same aisle, same sentence as the company on your left. You showed up as one more vendor in a hall that did not need another one. The buyer walked past. That was the review.

Crowdstrike can show up with a 10x10 and be fine. Your startup can't. Not if you are trying to break through all the agentic, autonomous AI SOC noise in a space like Black Hat. Every booth is already shouting a cousin of the same sentence. The companies attendees have an opinion about can afford to whisper. A startup cannot. If your presence doesn't change the walk down that aisle, you rented carpet and called it demand.

Going big is a decision you make before you sign, not a vibe you add once the booth diagram is locked. It means the space, the activations, the thing people are still talking about when they leave the hall. It means you were hard to miss. That costs a ton of money. The cost is the strategy. If the number makes you flinch, trust the flinch and stay home. Spend it somewhere you can be unmistakable, even if that somewhere is smaller than Vegas.

There's another way to light the same budget on fire, and it wears a lanyard to a different kind of show.

People go to Dreamforce or Unbound to take a mini vacation. I'd love to see Tom Brady and vibe to Usher, but I've got real work to do. Most of you who go to these shows stay out late, sleep in, and check a box by going to a session or two and posting about them. And don't forget to max out that expense account.

The appeal is obvious. The production is huge. Your friends are there. Nobody is a villain for wanting a few days of that. The problem starts when the vacation has to come home dressed as ROI.

"But Tom, it's all about the hallway conversations" is a cope.

A hallway is work when you flew in to have specific conversations. A customer you owe a real answer. An account you've been trying to get in front of. A partner, a hire, a meeting with a name on it. If you can't name those before you get on the plane, you are hoping the building does your job. It will not. You'll have a great time and report that the show felt important. Felt important is not an ROI story.

It's fine to take a mini work vacation. Say so. This job runs hot, and a company sending you somewhere fun so you can see your people is a sane thing to want. What I won't cosign is the speech about massive ROI from hearing Aziz Ansari or Reese Witherspoon ramble about things that have nothing to do with your job. Enjoy it. Leave it off the slide.

Put the two shows next to each other and the policy writes itself.

When the event is a trade show, your buyers are on the floor, and the category is screaming over itself, go big or don't go. A startup booth that blends in is a donation to the venue. Dominance is the thing you are buying. A logo on a floor plan is not dominance.

When the event is a spectacle, a celebrity keynote, and a reason to stay out late, go if you want the vacation. Own it. Expense it like what it is. Skip the recap that pretends a session and a post were ROI.

The version that fails is the average of the two. A little booth. A little party. A little content. A post. Then a conclusion that events don't work. Events work when you are willing to be unmistakable. They also "work" when you are honest that you went for the Usher set. Pick one before you book the flight.
