The biggest misconception about the CMO role is that the CMO is the best marketer at the company.

I'm certainly not the best marketer at incident.io.

I have experience, and I have instincts, and both help. That doesn't mean I'm always right. I've seen lots of things work and lots of things fail, and every situation is different. The thing that worked last time worked in a set of conditions: that product, that category, that budget, those competitors, that year. Take the lesson with you. Don't drag the tactic along like it's physics. The minute you do, you stop hearing the people who are closer to this market than your memory is.

I'm good at a lot of things. I am not the best on my team at any of them, other than maybe hacking around with Claude Code.

If you run marketing, sit with that, because it is the job and almost nobody describes it that way. If I were the best writer, the best product marketer, the best brand person, the best demand marketer, and the best marketing engineer on the team, I would have hired a group whose ceiling is me. That is a small ceiling. The point of a team is that other people are better at the craft than the person who hired them. My job is not to win the craft. My job is to make it possible for them to do the best work of their career.

What that looks like, day to day, is simpler than the keynote version of the CMO job. Empower the people who are smarter than me. Remove roadblocks. Hire great colleagues for them. Provide advice and guidance when they ask.

Empower means they own the work. The positioning, the page, the campaign, the experiment. I can have a point of view. I should. Experience is supposed to be good for something. It is not a required checkpoint on every sentence. Sharp people get average fast when their judgment has to route through yours before anything ships. You hired them to decide. Let them decide.

Roadblocks are where a CMO earns the seat. A decision nobody will make. A dependency on another team that has been stuck long enough to stall the work. A tool the team needs that dies because nobody senior will spend the capital to buy it. Feedback from above that landed sideways and froze a project that was fine. I am more useful clearing that than I am rewriting a headline someone else should own. "Remove roadblocks" sounds soft on a slide. In the week, it is most of the job.

Hiring is the part I can't do once and forget. The colleagues I bring in are the colleagues they have to work with every day. Hire people who won't threaten your taste, and the team gets polite and slow, and everything important waits on you. Hire someone who is obviously better than you at the thing, and your job changes in a way that should feel uncomfortable. Good. Stay out of their way, except when they want a second set of eyes.

"When they ask" is the clause I'd underline. Guidance from the CMO feels like a decision even when you swear you were just thinking out loud. People can hear the difference between "here's what I've seen, do what you want" and "here's what I've seen, and I'll be disappointed if you ignore it." I want the first one. Advice nobody asked for, delivered from the top, is a veto with better manners.

I've been wrong often enough to stop treating instincts as the plan. I'll tell you what I've watched work and what I've watched fail. Then the person closest to the problem decides, because every situation is different and they can see this one.

Companies keep the misconception alive by promoting the best marketer and waiting for the marketing to get better. What they often get is a slower team. You took your best practitioner off the work and turned them into a reviewer. There are stages of a company where the CMO is still doing the craft, and I still hack around. That is not the same thing as being the best marketer in the building, and it is a bad description of the job once people better than you are in the seats.

Be useful. Hire them. Unblock them. Get out of the draft unless they pull you in. Leave "best marketer at the company" to someone who still does the job every day.
